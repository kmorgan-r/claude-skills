# Europe Allows Birjand Saffron, America Bans It

**Mahdiyeh's father can lawfully sell his saffron and barberries in Europe, but not in the United States, and not on Amazon in either place.** EU sanctions on Iran were reinstated in September 2025. They ban oil, petrochemicals, gas, gold and weapons-related goods, but not food. A non-US person living in the EU can therefore import Birjand saffron and zereshk through an EU-registered food business, provided the label says "Iran", no sanctioned person or bank touches the deal, and payments follow EU notification rules. The US position is the opposite. Importing Iranian-grown food is banned by regulation and by a 2010 statute that covers goods sent "directly or indirectly". OFAC, the US Treasury's sanctions office, now presumes it will deny every Iran license request. The ban also follows any "US person" — a US citizen or green-card holder anywhere in the world, or anyone physically in the US — even into sales made only in Europe. Sending the goods to the family in Turkey first is lawful, and in 2026 it is practically the only way out of Iran, but it does not change their Iranian origin. Sorting, drying, repacking or grinding in Turkey leaves them Iranian, and relabelling them as Turkish is sanctions evasion and customs fraud. Only a genuinely new food made by a non-US company in Turkey, such as a saffron candy, has a narrow and untested path into the US. Amazon's global policy bars items "originating in" Iran, and so do Etsy, eBay and Stripe. That leaves two realistic channels: an own webshop using an EU payment provider, and wholesale to European shops. The hardest obstacles are practical rather than legal. European banks routinely refuse Iran payments even when the law allows them. The farmer needs a licensed Iranian exporter. And the 2026 war and US naval blockade have left one congested road crossing into Turkey as the main way out.

*Two notes before reading.* The question mentioned "Burberry", which is a British fashion brand. This report assumes it meant barberries (zereshk), the dried berry that South Khorasan dominates worldwide. This report is research compiled from public sources as of 18 October 2026, not legal advice. Iran sanctions changed almost monthly in 2026, so a sanctions lawyer should review the final plan before any money moves or any goods ship.

## Your passport and address decide more than your shipping route

US sanctions turn on one question: is a person a "US person"? The Iran sanctions regulations (the Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations, or ITSR) define a US person as **any US citizen, any green-card holder, any company organised under US law (including its foreign branches), and "any person in the United States"** ([eCFR 31 CFR 560.314](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-C/section-560.314)). This status travels with the person. **A US person "wherever located" may not engage in "any transaction or dealing" in Iranian-origin goods. The rule names purchasing, selling, transporting, brokering, financing and facilitating** ([eCFR 560.206](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.206)). That wording has no exception for sales made only in Europe. A US person also may not help a foreign person do what the US person cannot do ([eCFR 560.208](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.208)). OFAC treats even passing along purchase orders or business opportunities as prohibited help ([eCFR 560.417](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-D/section-560.417)). A foreign company "owned or controlled by a United States person" is bound the same way, and the US owner can be penalised for what the company does ([eCFR 560.215](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.215); [eCFR 560.105](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-A/section-560.105)).

Two consequences are easy to miss. First, a partner who is neither a US citizen nor a green-card holder still becomes a US person while physically in the United States, so handling orders during a US visit would bring that partner inside the ban. Second, setting up a US company, for example an LLC to sell on Amazon.com, creates a new US person that cannot touch the goods.

EU law works differently. It binds people and companies in the EU ([Regulation 267/2012](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2012/267/oj)). As the next sections show, it does not ban the product at all; it controls who you deal with and how you pay. Being Iranian is not the same as being sanctioned under either system, because sanctions lists name particular people, companies and banks. The table shows how the answer changes with the couple's status.

| Situation | Selling Birjand saffron and barberries in the EU | Selling them into the US | Paying the farmer in Iran |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neither partner is a US person, and both live in the EU | **Lawful.** Iranian origin must be stated, EU food rules met, and no listed people or banks involved. | **Prohibited.** Shipping to US buyers or Amazon US makes or "causes" a banned import. | Lawful in principle under EU food-payment rules, but banks often refuse. |
| One partner is a US person (a US citizen or green-card holder, even one living in the EU) and the other is not | The non-US partner may run it alone if there is no US link. The US partner may not own, fund, manage, advise, introduce customers or help. A company the US partner owns or controls is barred. | **Prohibited.** | The US partner may not pay anything to Iran. The non-US partner follows EU rules. |
| Both partners are US persons, or the business is run from inside the US | **Prohibited**, even for EU-only sales. | **Prohibited.** | **Prohibited.** The family-remittance license has been suspended since 24 August 2026. |
| The Turkish relatives (non-US persons) act as importer, packer or manufacturer | They may import, pack and re-export to the EU openly, with Iranian origin stated. | They may not send the raw goods to the US. A genuinely new product they make is a narrow exception that needs a ruling. | Turkish bank payments to Iran face heavy de-risking. No US dollars may be used. |

The first job follows from the table. Before anything else, the couple should establish in writing whether either of them is a US citizen (including dual citizenship), holds a green card, lives in the US, or owns a US company. If one partner is a US person, that partner must stay entirely outside the business: no ownership, money, advice, introductions or customer service. A US sanctions lawyer should advise on how to wall that partner off when the couple share household finances, because this research found no guidance on that point.

## America bans the farmer's products in every raw form, by any route

### Two layers of law close the door, and a 2010 statute locks it

The ITSR bans importing into the United States "any goods or services of Iranian origin" ([eCFR 560.201](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.201)). It defines Iranian-origin goods as anything "grown, produced, manufactured, extracted, or processed in Iran" ([eCFR 560.306](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-C/section-560.306)). Saffron and barberries grown in Birjand are Iranian-origin goods, and nothing done to them later in Turkey or Spain erases that. Congress wrote the same ban into a statute in 2010, the Comprehensive Iran Sanctions, Accountability, and Divestment Act (CISADA): **"no good or service of Iranian origin may be imported directly or indirectly into the United States"** ([22 U.S.C. 8512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/8512)). OFAC's long-standing guide says the same: Iranian goods may not be imported "either directly or through third countries" ([OFAC Iran overview](https://ofac.treasury.gov/system/files/126/iran.pdf)). The ban applies to whoever brings the goods in. A non-US seller in Europe who ships to American customers, or sends stock to an Amazon US warehouse, makes or "causes" a banned import, and causing a violation is itself unlawful ([50 U.S.C. 1705](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/50/1705); [eCFR 560.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.203)).

The door has opened before, but never easily. In April 2000 the US allowed Americans to import Iranian "dried fruits, nuts, and caviar" ([Federal Register, 3 May 2000](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2000/05/03/00-11009/iranian-transactions-regulations-licensing-of-imports-of-and-dealings-in-certain-iranian-origin)). That permission was revoked on 29 September 2010 ([75 FR 59611](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2010/09/28/2010-24211/iranian-transactions-regulations)). It reopened in January 2016 under the nuclear deal ([81 FR 3330](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-01-21/html/2016-01227.htm)). It closed again after a wind-down that ended on 6 August 2018 ([83 FR 30335](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2018/06/28/2018-13939/iranian-transactions-and-sanctions-regulations)).

Reopening now takes more than an OFAC decision. CISADA bars any exception for commercial imports of Iranian foodstuffs unless the President issues a regulation and certifies to Congress that it is in the national interest ([22 U.S.C. 8512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/8512)). Saffron (US tariff heading 0910.20) and dried barberries (heading 0813.40.15, which names barberries) both fall in the food chapters that rule covers ([19 U.S.C. 1304](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1304); [CBP ruling NY I87303](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/I87303)). On top of that, **since 10 September 2026 OFAC presumes it will deny Iran-related license requests. It grants them only "as required by law or in exceptional and urgent circumstances, such as risk to life, limb, or environmental safety"** ([OFAC Statement of Licensing Policy](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936916/download?inline)). The Iran general licenses OFAC issued in 2026 covered oil cargoes and wind-downs, never food ([91 FR 35141](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/10/2026-11614/publication-of-iran-related-web-general-licenses-u-and-v); [91 FR 61758](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/30/2026-20010/publication-of-iran-related-web-general-licenses-x-and-x1); [91 FR 57795](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/11/2026-18576/publication-of-iran-related-web-general-licenses-cc-and-dd)). Applying for a license to sell saffron in America is not a realistic plan.

### Penalties scale with value, and 2026 enforcement targets family ties

The research notes cite the penalty rules in two different places, and readers may see both. Some sources, including the Cornell mirror of 31 CFR 560.701, still show the penalty figures inside the Iran regulations ([LII 560.701](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/560.701)). The official eCFR, current to 15 October 2026, shows that OFAC moved penalties for all programs into a new 31 CFR Part 505, effective 25 September 2026, and deleted §560.701 on 30 September 2026 ([91 FR 61727](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/30/2026-20007/removing-duplicative-penalties-information-and-reorganizing-certain-parts)). The same change moved the detention-and-seizure rule from §560.702 to §560.105(c). **The safer reading is to cite Part 505.** The dollar figures are the same in both texts, so the conflict changes the citation, not the exposure.

**The maximum civil penalty is the greater of $377,700 per violation or twice the value of the transaction. A willful violation can bring a fine of $1,000,000 and 20 years in prison** ([91 FR 60821](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/25/2026-19678/sanctions-penalties-regulations)). Because of the "greater of" formula, a $2,000 saffron order carries the same $377,700 maximum as a $200,000 container. The government has **10 years** to bring a case ([50 U.S.C. 1705](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/50/1705)). Unlicensed Iranian shipments are detained and can be seized and forfeited ([eCFR 560.105](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-A/section-560.105)). Declaring Iranian saffron as Turkish, Afghan or Spanish at the US border adds customs crimes on top. False statements on entry carry up to two years in prison ([18 U.S.C. 542](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/542)). Smuggling carries up to 20 years ([18 U.S.C. 545](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/545)). Removing or altering origin markings to hide them brings fines of up to $100,000 for a first offence ([19 U.S.C. 1304](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1304)).

This research found no US case involving saffron or barberries, but the 2026 enforcement pattern is clear. On 24 August 2026 the Treasury launched "Operation Economic Outcast", which Secretary Bessent said aims to "sever every economic lifeline that sustains this tyrannical regime" ([Treasury press release](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0613)). On 10 September 2026 an individual US person paid **$1,427,230** to settle 39 apparent violations, including receiving Iranian-origin dividends and buying property in Iran. OFAC called the conduct "egregious", and the FBI took part ([OFAC enforcement release](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936911/download?inline)). One law firm reads the case as showing that dual nationals and people with family ties to Iran are now an enforcement focus ([Foley & Lardner](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/10/september-2026-u-s-sanctions-roundup-operation-economic-outcast-intensifies-against-iran-cuba-rules-tighten-and-ofac-overhauls-its-regulations/)). In May 2026 Adani Enterprises paid **$275 million** for causing violations by US persons, after a Dubai supplier passed off Iranian gas as "Omani and Iraqi-origin" ([Akrivis Law Group](https://www.akrivislaw.com/news-insights/legal-update-ofac-settles-at-275-million-civil-penalty-with-india-based-adani-enterprises-limited-over-iranian-origin-gas-shipments)). In 2022 a Hong Kong company paid $5.2 million because its US-dollar payments for Iranian-origin plastic cleared through US banks ([OFAC, Sojitz](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20220111_33)). The lesson for a small family business is that false-origin paperwork and dollar payments are exactly what investigators trace.

### Personal-use exceptions cannot stock a shop

A few narrow exceptions exist, and none can support a business. A relative may mail a gift worth **$100 or less** if it is "of a type and in quantities normally given as gifts between individuals" ([eCFR 560.506](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-E/section-560.506)). Travellers may bring saffron in accompanied baggage for personal use ([eCFR 560.210](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.210)). People moving to the US may bring used household effects "not intended for any other person or for sale" ([eCFR 560.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-E/section-560.524)). Splitting stock into a stream of $100 "gifts" would be evasion ([eCFR 560.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.203)). One 2026 change sometimes causes confusion: the food-and-medicine exemption added on 30 September 2026 covers only food going *to* Iran, not Iranian food coming out ([91 FR 61759](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/30/2026-19978/iranian-transactions-and-sanctions-regulations)).

## Europe allows the trade if the label tells the truth

### EU sanctions target people and payments, not saffron

The EU reinstated its Iran sanctions on 29–30 September 2025, after the UN "snapback" ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/eu-reintroduces-sanctions-against-iran-following-un-snapback)). The amended Regulation 267/2012 bans imports of crude oil and petroleum products, petrochemicals, natural gas, nuclear- and missile-related goods, and gold, precious metals and diamonds from the Iranian state ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975)). **Food appears in the regulation only as a reason for exemptions**, for example "transfers regarding foodstuffs" ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975)). Saffron (EU code 0910 20) and dried barberries (0813) are not on any banned list. The trade is real and continuing. **Spain imported 39,649 kg of saffron from Iran in 2024, worth $50.7 million, about 87% of its saffron imports by weight** ([World Bank WITS](https://wits.worldbank.org/trade/comtrade/en/country/ESP/year/2024/tradeflow/Imports/partner/ALL/product/091020)). EU imports from Iran are "dominated by pistachios and other food products" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/08/25/what-the-new-us-sanctions-on-iran-mean-for-european-businesses)).

The legal risk lies with the people and banks involved. The EU freezes the assets of more than 50 individuals and close to 200 entities, including major Iranian banks, energy firms and shipping companies ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/eu-reintroduces-sanctions-against-iran-following-un-snapback)). Since 19 February 2026 the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) has been on the EU terrorist list, which bars making funds available to it ([Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/02/19/eu-terrorist-list-council-designates-the-islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps-as-a-terrorist-organisation/)). No money may reach a listed person "directly or indirectly" ([Regulation 267/2012](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2012/267/oj)). Knowingly taking part in a scheme to get around the freezes or the payment rules is a separate offence ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975)).

In practice, the couple must check every party in the chain against the EU sanctions list and keep written records. That means the grower, any Iranian packer and exporter, the freight forwarder, any Turkish intermediary and every bank. This research found no EU Iran sanctions act after 24 July 2026. Check the EU Sanctions Map before relying on this report ([EU Sanctions Map](https://www.sanctionsmap.eu); [Council timeline](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/sanctions-against-iran/timeline-eu-sanctions-against-iran/)).

### Customs: low duties, and an Iranian origin that cannot be washed out

Whole saffron (CN 0910 20 10) enters the EU at **0% duty**. Crushed or ground saffron (0910 20 90) pays **8.5%** ([The Trade Hub](https://www.thetradehub.eu/en/customs/search?q=09102010); [TARIC Support](https://www.taricsupport.com/nomenclature/en/0910201000.html)). Dried barberries most likely fall under the "other dried fruit" line 0813 40 95, but the duty rate was not confirmed. A Binding Tariff Information ruling from customs would settle it ([TARIC Support](https://www.taricsupport.com/nomenclature/en/0813409580.html)).

Origin is fixed at harvest. EU rules treat "vegetable products harvested there" as wholly obtained in that country. They list drying, sorting, washing, cutting, repacking and labelling as "minimal operations" that never change origin ([Delegated Regulation 2015/2446](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32015R2446)). Declaring Turkey or Spain as the origin on an EU import declaration would be a false customs declaration. It would also gain nothing, because there is no EU ban to escape.

One 2026 change shapes the business model. **Since 1 July 2026, low-value parcels (up to €150) arriving from outside the EU pay a €3 customs duty per item** ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/ensuring-fairness-and-safety-eur3-customs-duty-low-value-parcels-2026-06-29_en)). That makes it costly to post individual orders from Turkey to EU customers. Importing in bulk through an EU business and shipping orders from inside the EU avoids the charge.

### Food law is the real gatekeeper

Iranian saffron and barberries are **not on the EU list of foods that get extra border checks**. The January 2026 revision of that list names Iran only for pistachios ([Regulation 2026/194](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202600194)). These goods therefore enter under ordinary risk-based checks, but the importer is still fully responsible for them. The importer must register as a food business with the local food authority ([Regulation 852/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2004/852/oj)), be able to trace every lot back to its supplier ([Regulation 178/2002](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2002/178/oj)), and meet EU contaminant and pesticide limits. The contaminant limits for dried fruit sold to consumers are **2.0 µg/kg for aflatoxin B1, 4.0 µg/kg for total aflatoxins, and 2.0 µg/kg for ochratoxin A**. Dried spices carry a 15 µg/kg ochratoxin A limit ([Regulation 2023/915](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02023R0915-20250701)). Where no specific pesticide limit has been set, the default is 0.01 mg/kg ([Regulation 396/2005](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2005/396/oj)).

These limits bite in practice. An Iranian survey found aflatoxins in 30% of barberry samples and ochratoxin A in 26.7% ([ResearchGate barberry survey](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273619652_A_Survey_of_Fungal_Contamination_Associated_with_Barberry_Berberis_spp_in_Iran)), so poorly dried or stored barberries can fail at the border. For saffron the main risk is fraud: synthetic dyes, safflower and added plant parts, with one review estimating that 5–8% of production is adulterated ([ResearchGate saffron review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279937406_Saffron_and_various_fraud_manners_in_its_production_and_trades)). The quality benchmark buyers expect is ISO 3632 grading ([ITC](https://intracen.org/es/media/2507)). Allergens are a third risk. In May 2026 Italy reported Iranian saffron with an undeclared nut allergen to the EU's food rapid-alert system, which suggests packing lines shared with pistachios ([Agrolab RASFF summary](https://agrolab.com/en/news/food-news/6476-warnmeldungen-rasff-lebensmittel-radar-06-26-en.html)).

On labels, honesty is a legal requirement. EU law makes country of origin mandatory where leaving it out "might mislead the consumer" ([Regulation 1169/2011](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj)). If a pack names one origin while its main ingredient comes from another, both must be stated ([Regulation 2018/775](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2018/775/oj)). Spain's "Azafrán de La Mancha" is a protected name, and imitating or evoking it is prohibited ([Regulation 2024/1143](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1143/oj)). That protection is heavily abused. Only **349 kg** of La Mancha saffron was certified in 2024 ([ESdiario](https://www.esdiario.com/economia/260413/184891/producto-netamente-espanol-beneficiar-conflicto-iran.html)). In 2021 Spanish police broke up a 17-person ring that sold bulked-out Iranian saffron as La Mancha saffron and seized more than two tonnes ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2021/05/07/saffron-gang-fraud/); [OCCRP](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/gang-made-millions-selling-fake-saffron-as-high-quality-product)). For the couple, this fraud is also an opening. Openly labelled Birjand saffron, tested and traceable to one family farm, tells a story that relabelled "Spanish" saffron cannot.

## Turkey works as a hub for Europe, not a back door to America

### Shipping through Turkey is lawful and changes nothing about origin

Nothing in Turkish law stops the family from receiving the goods. Turkey already imports saffron from "İran, Afganistan, İspanya ve Özbekistan" ([BAKA feasibility report](https://baka.gov.tr/assets/upload/dosyalar/safran.pdf)). An Iran–Turkey preferential trade agreement, in force since January 2015, gives Iran tariff cuts on 138 farm-product tariff lines ([ADASO analysis](https://adaso.org.tr/Content/WebDosyalar/Yayinlar/DisTicaretRaporlari/T%C3%9CRK%C4%B0YE%20%20%C4%B0RAN%20TERC%C4%B0HL%C4%B0%20T%C4%B0CARET%20ANLA%C5%9EMASI-ANAL%C4%B0Z-.pdf)). Turkey applied the reinstated UN measures in October 2025 ([Export Compliance Daily](https://exportcompliancedaily.com/news/2025/10/07/Turkey-Announces-Snapback-Sanctions-Against-Iran-2510060013)). Those measures target nuclear and missile matters and listed entities, not food. Turkey has also said in the past that it is not obliged to follow sanctions imposed outside the UN ([VOA, 2010](https://www.voanews.com/a/wait-and-see-game-for-turkeys-enforcement-of-un-sanctions-on-iran-96816624/120095.html)).

For sales in Europe, the research supports three lawful set-ups. In the simplest, the goods cross Turkey in sealed transit to an EU importer, which declares Iran as the origin. In the second, the relatives run a Turkish company that imports, sorts, tests and packs the goods, then exports them to the EU. Iran is stated as the origin on both the Turkish certificate of origin and the EU import declaration, and the pack reads "Origin: Iran; packed in Türkiye", never "Product of Türkiye". In the third, a Turkish producer makes a genuinely new product, as described below. Turkish chambers of commerce issue certificates of origin ([Lires customs guide](https://www.lires.com.tr/post/g%C3%BCmr%C3%BCkte-men%C5%9Fe-kurallar%C4%B1-ve-ispat-belgeleri-rehberi-eur-1-a-tr-ve-men%C5%9Fe-%C5%9Fahadetnamesi)). Asking a chamber to certify Birjand saffron as Turkish would mean making a false statement to a public body. Turkey offers no EU tariff advantage either. Whole saffron already enters the EU at 0%. Iranian inputs also cannot earn EU–Turkey trade preferences, because the Turkish A.TR movement certificate "is not a proof of origin" ([Irish Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/ga/customs/documents/atr-guide.pdf)).

For America, Turkey changes nothing, because CISADA also covers goods imported "indirectly". Turkey also brings its own 2026 risks. On 4 September 2026 OFAC blacklisted Turkey's Golden Global Yatırım Bankası for moving Iranian money ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/04/iran-turkey-sanctions-economy-bessent.html)). The Treasury has said that countries hosting Iran-related networks "will be given a defined timeline to shut down" that activity ([Treasury press release](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0613)). The relatives must keep the US dollar out of every payment. They also must not sell the raw goods to anyone shipping to the US. Either step risks "causing" a US violation of the kind that cost Adani $275 million.

### Only genuine manufacturing changes origin, and it matters only for America

For EU sales, processing is legally unnecessary, because Iranian saffron may be sold in the EU as it is. For US sales, everything depends on one regulation, 31 CFR 560.407. It allows goods containing Iranian raw materials into the US if those materials were "incorporated into manufactured products or substantially transformed in a third country **by a person other than a United States person**". Dealings in Iranian goods that were not so transformed "are prohibited" ([eCFR 560.407](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-D/section-560.407)).

US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) decides origin by asking whether "a new and different article" has emerged, with a "distinctive name, character, or use" ([CBP ruling NY N030426](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/N030426)). Its rulings point against products like saffron. Indian pepper ground and sterilised in Vietnam stayed Indian ([CBP NY N030426](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/N030426)). Raw nuts, including Iranian pistachios, roasted and packed in Hong Kong did not change origin, although honey-roasted and mustard peanuts did ([CBP NY I83434](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/I83434)). In January 2026, fresh Chinese chilies dried and ground in Korea did change origin ([CBP NY N356879](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/N356879)), but that case started from fresh produce. Saffron arrives already dried, so the pepper ruling is the closer match. Mixing that "leaves the identity of the article intact" does not count either ([CBP NY N281444](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/N281444)). EU rules point the same way. A spice mixture takes the origin of the ingredients making up more than 50% of its weight, and sugar confectionery takes the origin of the country where it is made ([Delegated Regulation 2015/2446](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32015R2446)). The table applies these rules to products the family might make in Turkey. No product-specific ruling exists for any of them, so every entry is a reasoned estimate.

| Product made in Turkey from Birjand inputs | Likely EU origin | Likely US status | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole saffron, sorted and repacked | Iran | Prohibited | Repacking is a "minimal operation" |
| Ground saffron | Probably Iran; get an EU origin ruling | Prohibited | Grinding a dried spice did not change origin in the pepper ruling |
| Dried barberries, cleaned, re-dried and packed | Iran | Prohibited | Comparable to roasting nuts |
| Rice kit with a saffron sachet | Iran, for the saffron | Prohibited | Packing items together as a set does not change origin |
| Saffron tea blend, mostly Turkish tea | Turkey under the 50%-by-weight rule, unless the blend exists to avoid the rules | Uncertain; blending may be too minor | Needs a CBP ruling |
| Saffron candy, rock candy (nabat) or lokum | Turkey; confectionery takes the origin of where it is made | Strongest candidate if made by a non-US company | Needs a CBP ruling and US food-safety (FDA) compliance |
| Barberry jam, juice or syrup | Uncertain; the weight rule may point back to Iran | Plausibly a new product | Needs both EU and US origin rulings |
| Saffron extract | Likely a change of origin | Plausibly a new product | Heavy additive and colouring rules |

The commercial catch is that the US-lawful product is a Turkish food that happens to contain Iranian saffron. It is not "Persian saffron". As the next subsection shows, saying so on the label creates its own problem.

### Where the rules collide: transformation versus "indirectly" versus Amazon

Three texts pull in different directions, and the research notes do not settle the conflict. OFAC's regulation (§560.407) lets in a product substantially transformed abroad by a non-US person. CISADA, a statute that outranks any regulation, bans Iranian-origin goods imported "directly or indirectly", and it forbids "exceptions" for commercial imports of Iranian foodstuffs without action by the President ([22 U.S.C. 8512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/8512)). Amazon's private policy bans items "imported from or originating in" Iran, without defining origin ([Amazon Seller Forums](https://sellercentral.amazon.com/seller-forums/discussions/t/06d05054-88f4-4ffa-84a2-87a8fef991a1)).

The better reading is that the two US texts fit together. §560.407 still appears in the current official regulations as of October 2026 ([eCFR 560.407](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-D/section-560.407)), so OFAC continues to apply it after CISADA. The most natural reading of "indirectly" covers Iranian goods routed through middlemen, not a new product whose customs origin is Turkish. Three facts still argue for caution. No OFAC guidance or CBP ruling applies §560.407 to food. The evasion rule bans any transaction that "has the purpose of evading or avoiding" the ban ([eCFR 560.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.203)), and a thin process set up mainly to get the father's saffron into America would invite that charge. And no source in this research addresses whether CISADA's bar on "exceptions" for Iranian foodstuffs limits how far §560.407 can stretch for food; only a lawyer can answer that.

**The safer reading is to treat §560.407 as available only when four conditions all hold: the product is a real manufactured food that makes commercial sense on its own, an independent non-US company makes it, a CBP binding origin ruling is obtained before the first shipment, and no US person is involved in buying, financing or directing the Iranian inputs.** If either partner is a US person, that partner may at most buy the finished product at arm's length.

Amazon is a separate gate, because its policy is a private rule that can be stricter than the law. EU labelling rules can require a saffron candy to say where its saffron comes from ([Regulation 2018/775](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2018/775/oj)). Amazon checks for Iranian links and asks sellers for certificates of origin ([Amazon Seller Forums](https://sellercentral.amazon.com/seller-forums/discussions/t/06d05054-88f4-4ffa-84a2-87a8fef991a1)). **The safer reading is that Amazon will treat any product presented as containing Iranian saffron or barberries as "originating in" Iran, whatever its customs origin.** Do not plan a transformed product around Amazon without first getting Amazon's written confirmation. Hiding the Iranian ingredient to get past Amazon's checks is not an option: it would breach the labelling rules above and Amazon's terms.

## Amazon and the big US platforms are closed; an own webshop is not

Amazon is the hardest "no" in the plan. **Amazon's Trade Control Laws policy states that "items imported from or originating in Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Syria, and the Crimea region of Ukraine are not allowed to be sold on Amazon."** Amazon staff resolve flagged listings by asking for "a certificate of origin or certificate of compliance" ([Amazon Seller Forums](https://sellercentral.amazon.com/seller-forums/discussions/t/06d05054-88f4-4ffa-84a2-87a8fef991a1)). This bans goods by where they come from, not only shipments to Iran. A lawyer who represents Amazon sellers warns that sellers who breach sanctions rules "can be and are suspended from Amazon all the time", even when they do so unknowingly ([TaxJar](https://www.taxjar.com/blog/amazon-sanctions-law)). Amazon has its own reasons to be strict: in 2020 OFAC fined it **$134,523** over sanctions-screening failures that included Iran ([OFAC Amazon settlement](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/44311/download?inline=)).

This research could not retrieve Amazon's live European policy page. A German-language summary describes the same ban on Amazon.de ([kuechenfibel.de](https://www.kuechenfibel.de/faq/in-welchem-land-ist-amazon-nicht-verfugbar)). Amazon's European storefronts are run by companies owned by a US company, so the US rule for US-owned foreign entities reaches them ([eCFR 560.215](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-B/section-560.215)). Listing Birjand saffron on Amazon.com would be a banned US import. Listing it on Amazon.de, .fr, .es or .it would breach Amazon's policy, even though EU law allows the sale. Grocery is also a restricted category on Amazon, open only to sellers Amazon approves after reviewing their documents ([Amazon seller blog](https://sell.amazon.com/blog/how-to-sell-groceries-amazon); [Jungle Scout](https://www.junglescout.com/resources/articles/how-to-sell-groceries-on-amazon/)).

The other big US-headquartered channels take the same line. Etsy "does not allow the sale of items that originate from sanctioned countries" ([Etsy Help](https://help.etsy.com/hc/en-us/articles/23786242550039-How-can-I-Follow-Etsy-s-Sanction-Policy)). eBay says "Goods from embargoed countries aren't allowed" and lists Iran ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/help/policies/prohibited-restricted-items/embargoed-goods-prohibited-countries-policy?id=4323)). Stripe prohibits selling "products or services originating from" Iran ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-gb-ca/legal/restricted-businesses)), which also closes Shopify Payments wherever Stripe processes them ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/legal/processor-list)). PayPal's policy could not be retrieved, but it is very likely closed for the same reasons. These platforms screen keywords aggressively. eBay sellers have reported listings removed for the word "Persian" alone ([eBay Community](https://community.ebay.com/t5/Selling/Policy-Enforcement-embargoed-countries-needs-to-learn-to-read-a/m-p/33672033)). Iranian-American groups have complained that Etsy removed US-made "Persian" items ([NIAC](https://niacouncil.org/niac-letter-regarding-etsys-u-s-sanctions-compliance-policies-discriminatory-treatment-towards-iranian-americans/)). Some EU marketplaces do the same: Sweden's Tradera lists Iran among restricted origins ([Tradera](https://www.tradera.com/support/gb/posts/varor-fran-sanktionerade-laender/)).

What remains open are channels without a US parent company. Adyen, a Dutch payment provider, publishes no country-specific ban, only a requirement to follow "Applicable Laws" ([Adyen](https://adyen.com/zh_CN/legal/list-restricted-prohibited)). This research found no policy text for Mollie, Klarna or Bol.com. **The realistic route to market is an own webshop using an EU-headquartered payment provider.** Add wholesale sales to Persian grocers, delis and restaurants, and to existing EU saffron importers. The couple should tell the payment provider and the bank where the product comes from when opening the account, because hiding it risks a frozen account if they find out later. The webshop should block delivery to US addresses. Some will ask about the EU Blocking Statute, which bars EU firms from complying with US Iran sanctions without authorisation ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/08/25/what-the-new-us-sanctions-on-iran-mean-for-european-businesses)). Nothing in this research suggests a small seller could use it to force a platform to accept listings, so it is a question for a lawyer, not a business plan.

## Paying the farmer is the hardest lawful step

For a non-US buyer in the EU, paying an Iranian supplier for food is legal within set limits. Since September 2025, transfers between EU banks and Iranian banks have been banned unless they fall under a listed purpose, and food is one of those purposes ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975)). **For food purchases, transfers under €10,000 need no filing. From €10,000 the bank must notify the authorities in advance. Transfers through an Iranian bank of €100,000 or more need prior authorisation** ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975); [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/eu-reintroduces-sanctions-against-iran-following-un-snapback)). The bank files the notice with the national authority; in Germany, that is the Bundesbank ([Bundesbank](https://www.bundesbank.de/de/service/finanzsanktionen/sanktionsregimes/-/iran-610216)). Payments that "appear to be linked" are added together. Splitting a €30,000 purchase into four €7,500 transfers to stay under the limit would itself break the rules ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/eu-reintroduces-sanctions-against-iran-following-un-snapback)). The receiving Iranian bank must not be a listed one. Banks whose assets are frozen include the Central Bank of Iran, Bank Melli, Bank Mellat, Bank Saderat, Sina Bank and the Europäisch-Iranische Handelsbank in Hamburg ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2025/10/reimposition-of-un-mandated-sanctions-against-iran-and-additional-eu-and-uk-sanctions)).

The law is not the bottleneck; the banks are. EU banks may not open new correspondent relationships with Iranian banks ([Regulation 2025/1975](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202501975)). A 2026 analysis calls the food exemptions "largely inert" because "tier-one Western banks routinely block them" ([Jerusalem Post](http://www.jpost.com/jerusalem-report/article-911501)). This research found no named EU bank that currently processes small food payments to Iran.

The workarounds carry their own risks. A Turkish company that buys from Iran and invoices the EU business is lawful only if it is fully transparent. Used to hide the Iranian supplier from an EU bank or to avoid notification, it becomes circumvention, and Turkish banks are under heavy US pressure after the Golden Global designation ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/04/iran-turkey-sanctions-economy-bessent.html)). In Germany, running a hawala service without a licence is a crime punishable by up to five years in prison, and the Federal Court of Justice confirmed convictions in 2025 ([Ferner Alsdorf](https://www.ferner-alsdorf.de/bundesgerichtshof-erneut-zur-strafbarkeit-von-hawala-systemen-2025/)). The sender is not prosecuted ([Bundestag](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/20/120/2012058.pdf)), but still risks account closure and confiscation. Many Iranian exchange houses are now US-sanctioned or owned by sanctioned banks ([Iran International](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202610071494)). Reports that Iran now lets exporters bring earnings home in cryptocurrency are unofficial ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/iran-central-bank-encourages-crypto-evade-sanctions)), and this research did not examine EU rules on paying that way, so it is not a recommended route.

For US persons, there is no lawful way to pay. Sending money to Iran counts as a banned export of financial services ([eCFR 560.427](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-560/subpart-D/section-560.427)). The general license for noncommercial family remittances was suspended on 24 August 2026 ([91 FR 54957](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/26/2026-17426/iranian-transactions-and-sanctions-regulations)), and it never covered payment for goods.

Iran adds a rule of its own. It requires exporters to bring their foreign-currency earnings back into the Iranian system. In 2026 the deadline for the previous year's obligations was extended to the end of Azar 1405, about 21 December 2026 ([Arman-e Kasb-o-Kar](https://armanekasbokar.ir/%D9%85%D9%87%D9%84%D8%AA-%D8%B1%D9%81%D8%B9-%D8%AA%D8%B9%D9%87%D8%AF-%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%B2%DB%8C-%D8%B3%D8%A7%D9%84-%DB%B1%DB%B4%DB%B0%DB%B4-%D8%AA%D8%A7-%D9%BE%D8%A7%DB%8C%D8%A7%D9%86-%D8%A2%D8%B0%D8%B1/)). Tehran also lets exporters clear some obligations by importing basic goods ([Rahbord-e Mo'aser](https://rahbordemoaser.ir/fa/news/309662/)). This obligation falls on the licensed exporter, not the farmer, but it shapes how and where the exporter will want to be paid.

## War and blockade leave one slow road out of Birjand

### The farmer needs a licensed exporter

Iranian law requires a **commercial card (kart-e bazargani)** for any commercial export. The Iran Chamber of Commerce issues the card and the Ministry of Commerce approves it ([Export-Import Regulations Act](https://old.fsvps.gov.ru/fsvps-docs/ru/importExport/iran/files/iran_law_import_export.pdf)). A small farmer without a card must sell through an exporter or company that has one. A saffron export passes a price review, a standard (quality) certificate, a health or phytosanitary certificate, a customs declaration and a physical inspection before customs issues the export licence ([Rahkar Tejarat](https://rahkartejarat.com/exporting/export-saffron/)). Iranian labs test saffron against both the national standard (INSO 259) and ISO 3632 ([University of Birjand](https://cv.birjand.ac.ir/hamami/en/articlesInConferences/50279)). An FAO project has been training labs accredited by the Iran Food and Drug Administration in the Khorasan provinces ([FAO](https://iran.un.org/en/download/185645/295888)). Barberry exports need a permit from the provincial agriculture organisation (Jihad-e Keshavarzi). South Khorasan issued permits for **140,782 kg in the first half of the Iranian year 1405, against 349,166 kg in the whole previous year**. Buyers included Turkey and Germany ([IRIB News](https://www.irib-news.ir/fa/news/5935397/)).

Some rules are unsettled, and the sources conflict. Iran abolished its saffron export duty in 2013 ([Global Trade Alert](https://globaltradealert.org/intervention/17744-iran-elimination-of-export-duty-on-saffron)), yet one freight forwarder still cites a 5% duty on bulk packs over 30 g ([Persian Forwarder](https://persianforwarder.com/blog/saffron-tariff/)). Exporters also describe restrictions on exporting saffron in bulk, but this research found no decree confirming them ([Rahkar Tejarat](https://rahkartejarat.com/exporting/export-saffron/)). The licensed exporter will know the current position. Carrying saffron in luggage is no substitute: Iranian passport holders may reportedly carry only 100 g ([Rahkar Tejarat](https://rahkartejarat.com/exporting/export-saffron/)).

### Sea, air and courier routes are shut

The 2026 war began on 28 February, when the US and Israel struck Iran, and Iran then blocked the Strait of Hormuz ([Wikipedia: 2026 blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_naval_blockade_of_Iran)). A first US naval blockade of Iranian ports ran from 13 April to 18 June 2026, although one source gives an earlier end date in late May. **A second blockade, covering "the entirety of the Iranian coastline", has run since 14 July 2026** ([Wikipedia: 2026 blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_naval_blockade_of_Iran); [Wikipedia: Hormuz crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis)). In October both the US and Iran claimed control of the strait amid attacks on ships ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/17/us-and-iran-both-claim-control-of-strait-of-hormuz-amid-attacks-on-vessels)). Air freight is also shut. The US sanctioned 27 Iranian airlines on 8 September 2026, and almost all flights to Turkey, the UAE and China were then cancelled ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/23/us-aviation-sanctions-disrupt-iran-flights-push-travellers-overland)). The EU aviation safety agency's conflict-zone warning for Iranian airspace runs to 16 November 2026 ([EASA](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/air-operations/czibs)). Couriers are out too: DHL has suspended service to Iran ([WES](https://www.wes.org/important-wes-notices/)).

That leaves the road to Turkey through the Bazargan–Gürbulak border crossing, which is jammed. **In October 2026 about 8,000 trucks were stranded at Bazargan, each costing about $175 a day. Freight from Turkey to Iran rose from about $1,500 to $7,900–8,000 per truck in under two months** ([Iran International](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202610114328)). In late September, queues stretched about 7 km and Iranian customs delays ran 12–25 days ([INVC](https://www.internationalnewsandviews.com/iran-trade-crisis-turkiye-border-truck-queues-us-blockade-2026-409762-2/)). Those freight prices are for trucks going into Iran, not out, but they show how disrupted the only open route is. This research found no prices for insuring Iranian cargo in wartime. Inside Iran, inflation reached 73.6% in September 2026, and the rial lost about 35% against the dollar after July ([Daily Qudrat, citing Reuters](https://en.dailyqudrat.pk/107711/)). Any price agreed with the farmer will be unstable.

### The numbers favour saffron, not barberries

Saffron can carry these costs. Wholesale saffron in Iran sold for **about 298–315 million toman per kg in October 2026, roughly $1,125–1,190 per kg** at the open-market exchange rate ([Nabz-e Gheymat](https://nabzgheymat.ir/%D9%82%DB%8C%D9%85%D8%AA-%D8%B2%D8%B9%D9%81%D8%B1%D8%A7%D9%86-%D8%A7%D9%85%D8%B1%D9%88%D8%B2-%DB%B1%DB%B9-%D9%85%D9%87%D8%B1-%DB%B1%DB%B4%DB%B0%DB%B5-%E2%94%82-%D8%AA%D9%81%D8%A7%D9%88%D8%AA-%D9%86/); [Pashizi](https://www.pashizi.com/en/currency/usd)). In Europe, top-grade saffron sells wholesale for about **€3,200–3,250 per kg** ([Ana Saffron](https://anasaffron.com/iranian-saffron-price/)), and retail prices run from $4 to $20 per gram depending on grade ([Raihan Saffron](https://raihansaffron.com/blogs/journal/saffron-price-guide)). At those prices five kilos are worth about €16,000 at European wholesale, so a small shared consignment can absorb high freight.

Barberries are the opposite. An Iranian retail site lists bulk barberries at about 710,000 toman per kg, under $3 at the open-market rate ([RedGolden](https://redgolden.net/buy-barberry/); [Pashizi](https://www.pashizi.com/en/currency/usd)). That figure is unreliable, but it shows that small barberry lots cannot absorb current freight costs unless they travel with other goods. South Khorasan grows more than 95% of the world's barberries ([Journal of Saffron Research](https://jsr.birjand.ac.ir/article_1170.html?lang=en)), so the product is distinctive. It makes most sense as a side line shipped together with the saffron.

## What is lawful, what is prohibited, and what needs a lawyer

The table below gathers the verdicts. "Lawful" means lawful under the rules described above, provided the conditions in the earlier sections are met. It does not mean that a bank, carrier or platform will agree to take part.

| Activity | Verdict | Main reason |
|---|---|---|
| Non-US persons in the EU importing Birjand saffron or barberries and selling them in the EU, labelled with Iranian origin | **Lawful** | No EU ban on Iranian food; food, label, customs and payment rules apply |
| Turkish relatives importing, sorting, testing and packing the goods, then exporting to the EU with Iran stated as the origin | **Lawful** | Turkish law permits it; minimal processing keeps Iranian origin |
| Own webshop using an EU payment provider, plus wholesale to EU shops and restaurants | **Lawful** (if the provider and bank accept the business) | No law bars it; provider policies vary |
| Paying the exporter from an EU bank for food, with bank notification at €10,000 or more | **Lawful** | EU food exemption; banks may still refuse |
| A genuine gift of $100 or less, or saffron in personal luggage, into the US | **Lawful** | Narrow personal-use exemptions |
| Selling the saffron or barberries to US customers in any raw, ground or repacked form, by any route | **Prohibited** | US import ban; CISADA "directly or indirectly" |
| Listing them on Amazon (any storefront), Etsy or eBay, or using Stripe or Shopify Payments | **Prohibited** (platform policy everywhere; also US law for US sales) | Platforms ban goods by origin |
| Any role for a US person, even in an EU-only business | **Prohibited** | §560.206, §560.208, §560.215 |
| Labelling or declaring the goods as Turkish, Spanish or Afghan, or evoking La Mancha | **Prohibited everywhere** | Customs fraud, food-label fraud, sanctions evasion |

| A new product (for example, saffron candy) made by a non-US Turkish company for the US market | **Needs a lawyer** | §560.407 is untested for food; CBP ruling first; open question under CISADA |
| Walling off a partner who is a US person | **Needs a lawyer** | Where ownership, control and facilitation lines fall |
| Using a Turkish company to invoice the EU business | **Needs a lawyer** | Lawful only if fully transparent |
| Setting up the EU company, opening bank accounts, and screening Iranian counterparties for IRGC links | **Needs a lawyer or compliance adviser** | Exposure to asset freezes and the terrorist listing |
| Tariff classification of barberries; origin of ground saffron or barberry jam | **Needs a customs ruling** | Not confirmed by this research |
| Iranian export paperwork and currency-return obligations | **Needs an Iranian customs broker or lawyer** | Rules changing in 2026 |

The order of the next steps matters, because the first answer decides whether the rest apply at all.

| Order | Step | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish each partner's status: any US citizenship (including dual), green card, US residence or US company. Do no business work while in the US. | If either partner is a US person, US law bars that partner from the business entirely |
| 2 | If either partner is a US person, see a US sanctions lawyer before taking any other step. | The ban applies worldwide, and 2026 enforcement targets family ties to Iran |
| 3 | Decide to sell in the EU only. Remove the US and Amazon from the plan, and block US delivery in the webshop. | Both are closed for raw saffron and barberries |
| 4 | Form an EU business owned and run by the non-US partner. Register it with the local food authority, get an EU customs number (EORI), and check national VAT. | The importer of record carries the food-law duties |

| 6 | Test samples at an EU-accredited lab. For saffron: ISO 3632 grade, dyes and adulterants, pesticides and nut allergens. For barberries: aflatoxins, ochratoxin A, sulphites and pesticides. | Contaminants and fraud are the main border and market risks |

| 9 | Design honest labels: "Origin: Iran (Birjand, South Khorasan)", "packed in Türkiye" where it applies, an allergen statement, and no La Mancha imagery. | Honest labels are both a legal duty and the selling point |

## Conclusion

The research turns the couple's question around. "Can we sell on Amazon in Europe and the US?" becomes "Can we build an honest Persian-saffron business in Europe without Amazon?" — and the answer to that second question is yes. In the EU the law is permissive. What stands in the way are private gatekeepers and physical ones: US-owned platforms, cautious banks and a single jammed border crossing. Each of those tightened during 2026, and none is a legal ban. That means effort spent on bank relationships, testing and logistics pays off more than effort spent looking for legal loopholes. Honest origin is also more than a legal duty. In a market where much "Spanish" saffron is relabelled Iranian, a traceable single-farm Birjand product is a real competitive advantage.

The US door is closed by statute, not just by policy. It opened twice before, in 2000 and 2016, both times during diplomatic thaws, and each time only after formal government action. A return to the US market therefore depends on the course of US policy toward Iran; it cannot be part of a business plan. The one fact that would change this report most is not yet known: whether either partner is a US person. Establishing that costs nothing, and it should come before any shipment, bank account or label design.
